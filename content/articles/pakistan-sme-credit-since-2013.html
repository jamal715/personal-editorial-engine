Credit and the real economy

Of all the bank credit that goes to private businesses in Pakistan, how much reaches small and medium enterprises, through which types of finance, and how has that changed since 2013?

About one rupee in ten: Rs1,046bn of Rs9,811bn in June 2026, or 10.7%, against 9.5% in December 2013. Most of it now comes as long-term investment finance (53%) and working capital (43%); almost none as trade finance (5%). The share fell for a decade to 7.0% before rebounding sharply after 2022, driven almost entirely by long-term finance.

The answer in five points

  1. How much: 10.7% of business credit (Rs1,046bn of Rs9,811bn) reached SMEs in June 2026, against 9.5% in December 2013. The endpoints are similar; the path between them is not.
  2. Through which types: long-term (fixed investment) finance is 53% of SME credit, working capital 43%, trade finance 5%. SMEs hold 14.3% of all long-term business credit, 11.1% of working capital and 2.5% of trade finance.
  3. Change, 2013–2022: SMEs lost ground. Corporates took 93–97% of new business credit, and the SME share fell from 10.2% (Dec 2016) to 7.8% (Jun 2022).
  4. Change, since 2022: SMEs took 18.4% of new credit; SME credit grew 17.1% a year against 8.2% for all business credit. Long-term finance supplied 78% of that SME growth. SME trade finance shrank.
  5. Composition of change since 2013: SME long-term credit rose from Rs32bn to Rs553bn (from 12% to 53% of the SME book); working capital fell from 78% to 43% of it; SME penetration of trade finance fell from 4.4% to 2.5%.

2How much reaches SMEs

Business credit rose 3.4 times, from Rs2,869bn (December 2013) to Rs9,811bn (June 2026). SME credit rose 3.9 times, from Rs271bn to Rs1,046bn. The SME share was 9.5% at the start and 10.7% at the end, peaked at 10.2% in December 2016, and bottomed at 7.0% in December 2024. The December 2024 low reflects a one-quarter surge in corporate working-capital lending ahead of the year-end advance-to-deposit ratio (ADR) tax test. Excluding that quarter, the low is 7.1% (March 2023).

3Through which types of finance

3.1 By facility

  • Fixed investment (long-term finance): Rs553bn of SME credit, 52.8% of the SME book; SMEs hold 14.3% of all long-term business credit.
  • Working capital: Rs446bn, 42.6% of the SME book; SMEs hold 11.1% of working-capital credit; working capital was SMEs' highest-penetration facility until September 2025, when fixed investment overtook it.
  • Trade finance: Rs48bn, 4.6% of the SME book; SMEs hold 2.5% of trade finance.

December 2013 to June 2022. Corporates took 93% of new business credit before 2019 and 97% between 2019 and 2022. SME credit grew 9.8% and then 4.1% a year, below business credit in both sub-periods. The 2020–22 years include the Temporary Economic Refinance Facility (TERF): LTFF/TERF rose from Rs35bn (2013) to Rs629bn (June 2022), and subsidised schemes reached 19.5% of business credit (Figure 8). The SME share fell to 7.8% by June 2022.

June 2022 to June 2026. SMEs took 18.4% of the Rs2,659bn increase. SME credit grew 17.1% a year against 8.2% for business credit and 7.4% for corporate credit. SME fixed-investment credit grew 34.5% a year and supplied 78.3% of SME growth; SME trade finance fell 1.2% a year.

4.2 Long-term finance: from 3.7% to 14.3% of the market

SME fixed-investment credit rose from Rs32bn (December 2013) to Rs553bn (June 2026), a CAGR of 25.5%. It explains 67.1% of all SME credit growth since 2013. SME penetration of long-term finance was 5.5–7.0% from 2016 to 2023 and rose to 9.2% (December 2024) and 14.3% (June 2026), while corporate long-term credit growth slowed to 7.4% a year in 2022–26 from 14.2% in 2019–22.

4.3 Working capital: from 78% to 43% of the SME book

SME working capital rose from Rs213bn to Rs446bn (6.1% a year), slower than working capital overall (8.9%), so SME penetration fell from 15.3% to 11.1%. In December 2024 it dropped to 7.5%: the "working capital, other" line rose Rs1,594bn between September and December 2024, almost entirely corporate, and corporate credit fell back Rs1,013bn by March 2025.

4.4 Trade finance: from 4.4% to 2.5%

Trade finance tripled (Rs600bn → Rs1,927bn) while SME trade finance rose only from Rs26bn to Rs48bn, and fell after 2022. The main movement inside trade finance followed the July 2022 change to the Export Finance Scheme: EFS fell from Rs728bn (June 2022) to Rs333bn (June 2026) while commercial export finance rose from Rs149bn to Rs926bn. On the pro-rata estimate, SMEs account for about Rs23bn of the commercial export book.

4.5 Subsidised finance

Subsidised schemes rose from 8.6% of business credit (December 2013) to 19.5% (June 2022) and returned to 9.5% (June 2026). On the pro-rata estimate, about Rs62bn of the Rs432bn LTFF/TERF stock is SME credit; given TERF's focus on large projects (up to Rs5bn each), the true figure is likely lower.

6Reconciliation of the data

6.1 The compiled Excel file

The compiled monthly file (158 months, Feb 2013 – Mar 2026) was tested against two identities that must hold every month: the 13 types sum to the total, and SME plus corporate equals the total.

  • Stale values: foreign bills in July 2016 and May 2019 were exact copies of the previous month; each was replaced with the total minus the other 12 types.
  • Short total: August 2025 total was Rs826m short; both identities gave the same corrected value.
  • 2025 gap: January–June 2025 SME plus corporate exceeded the total by Rs13–57bn; resolved by the official file (6.3).
  • Wrong labels: "Short-term advances" is SBP small loans (working capital); "Small loans" is small loans (fixed investment); "Factoring" is inland bills purchased and discounted.

Correction: foreign bills, May 2019

Total business credit Rs5,231,256.6m − other 12 types Rs5,125,048.4m = Rs106,208.2m (the file showed Rs102,943.3m, April's value). SBP official archive: Rs106,208.21m.

6.2 The SME Finance Review: 42 PDF issues

  • Coverage: no PDF on the SBP site for December 2016 or December 2021; no issue for September 2015 or September 2016. These quarters come from the comparison columns of later issues.
  • Formats: 12–23 page reports (2015–16) and one-page data tables (2017 onward). Tables identified by title, as numbering changed (June 2017). Split digits in the PDF text ("3 67.25" = 367.25; "9 .51" = 9.51) repaired.
  • 2013–15: the facility and sector split exists only as chart labels. Each label was assigned to a quarter and series by its position on the page. Validation: the three facility values summed to the Table 1 SME total in 64 of 64 cases and matched exact tables in 18 of 18 overlapping quarters.
  • Revisions: where a quarter appears in several issues, the latest issue is used. Seven items were revised by more than 0.5%; the largest is December 2016 SME credit (Rs401.00bn → Rs394.65bn).

6.3 The SBP official archive

129 monthly sheets (December 2015 – August 2026, three column layouts) were compared with the compiled file: 96% of 1,984 cells identical; all three corrections in 6.1 matched to the decimal. In 2025, the compiled file's SME and corporate figures were correct and its total (and some types, chiefly LTFF/TERF) were provisional values later revised upward. The official archive is used from December 2015; the join is exact (difference 0.0).

6.4 The June 2019 reclassification

The archive holds June 2019 on the old industry classification and under ISIC Rev. 4. On the new basis SME credit is Rs48.4bn higher (Rs444.7bn → Rs493.1bn) and corporate credit Rs44.2bn lower, with no change in lending. The break is marked on every chart; growth is quoted for sub-periods that avoid it; a break-adjusted SME series is provided (Calc_Facility, column AH).

7Method and calculations

7.1 Conversions and alignment

  • Units: Source 1 (Rs million) ÷ 1,000 = Rs billion. Example: Rs9,811,334m → Rs9,811.3bn.
  • Frequency: quarter-end values (March, June, September, December), not averages or sums of months.
  • Facilities (SBP grouping, July 2022 layout):
    Trade finance = EFS + export finance other + import financing + import bills + foreign bills.
    Working capital = small loans (WC) + working capital other + inland bills.
    Fixed investment = LTFF/TERF + small loans (FI) + fixed investment other + construction + govt self-employment schemes.

7.2 The core identity

SMEf,t = SMEt(Source 1) × [ SMEf,t(Review) ÷ SMEtotal,t(Review) ]
Corporatef,t = Totalf,t(Source 1) − SMEf,t
Penetrationf,t = SMEf,t ÷ Totalf,t

SME levels come from Source 1, so that SME plus corporate equals the SBP total exactly; Source 2 supplies only the mix across facilities. The two SME series differ by up to 20% in level (Section 8.1), so using Review levels would push that difference into corporate credit.

  7. Check: 552.6 + 445.9 + 47.9 − 1,046.4 = 0 (AI58).

Contribution = ΔSegment ÷ ΔTotal between two dates

Calculations (workbook sheets Growth and Contribution)

  • SME CAGR, Dec 2013 → Jun 2026 (12.5 years): (1,046.4 ÷ 271.3)1/12.5 − 1 = 3.8570.08 − 1 = 11.4%.
  • Break-adjusted: ((1,046.4 − 48.4) ÷ 271.3)1/12.5 − 1 = 11.0%.
  • SME share of new credit, Jun 2022 → Jun 2026: (1,046.4 − 556.7) ÷ (9,811.3 − 7,152.7) = 489.7 ÷ 2,658.5 = 18.4%.
  • Fixed investment's share of SME growth, same period: (552.6 − 168.9) ÷ 489.7 = 78.3%.
  • SME growth in 2025: 952.5 ÷ 669.8 − 1 = +42.2%.
  • Commercial export finance CAGR: (925.7 ÷ 79.2)1/12.5 − 1 = 21.7%; share of total growth (925.7 − 79.2) ÷ 6,941.9 = 12.2%.

7.4 Mix and penetration

  • Mix = facility ÷ SME total. Example: 552.6 ÷ 1,046.4 = 52.8% of SME credit is fixed investment.
  • Penetration = SME ÷ facility total. A rise can come from SME growth or corporate decline, so it is read with levels.

7.5 Checks and sensitivity

  • SME by facility sums to SME total, and corporate by facility to corporate total: largest difference Rs0.01bn across 54 quarters.
  • No negative corporate value and no penetration above 50% in any quarter.
  • Inland bills counted as trade finance instead of working capital (June 2026): trade finance penetration 2.5% → 2.3%; working capital 11.1% → 11.5%.

7.6 Type-level SME estimate

SMEtype ≈ SMEfacility × Type ÷ Totalfacility

Example, fixed investment other, June 2026: 552.6 × 2,933.9 ÷ 3,873.6 ≈ Rs418.5bn. The allocation assumes SMEs use each type in the same proportion as all borrowers within the facility; SME use of large commercial long-term loans is probably overstated and of small loans and government schemes understated.

8Robustness of the results

8.1 Two official SME series

The gap ranges from −8.1% (December 2018) to +19.7% (September 2024) and was +15.4% in June 2026. It changes sign at the June 2019 reclassification and narrows every December. On the SME Review's level, the SME share of business credit in June 2026 would be 9.2% instead of 10.7%. The facility mix, and hence the answer to "through which types", is common to both.

8.2 The June 2019 reclassification

Rs48.4bn of the SME stock is a relabelling of borrowers, not lending. It raises the full-period SME CAGR from 11.0% to 11.4%.

8.3 Year-end lending

SME credit rose between September and December in all 13 years (average +15.9%, against +9.6% for corporates) and fell back by March in 12 of them (average −5.9%), consistent with year-end SME lending targets introduced in January 2016. December SME figures therefore overstate the underlying stock; June and September values are the cleaner comparison. In 2025 the March reversal did not occur (+2.5%).

8.4 The 2025 increase

Business credit fell 0.7% in 2025 while SME credit rose 42.2%. Both SBP series rose; SME borrowers rose from 184,000 (December 2024) to 303,000 (December 2025); the SME NPL ratio fell from 14% to 10%; the March 2026 reversal did not occur. Against this, borrowers rose 36% in one quarter (March–June 2025), the gap between the two series widened to 15%, and the SME regulations were revised twice in 2025. The direction of the 2025 change is firm; its size may include reclassification.

9Limitations

  1. Nominal values; part of every growth rate is inflation.
  2. Stocks, not flows; disbursements, loan terms and prices are not observed.
  3. Supply and demand cannot be separated.
  4. Levels depend on the SME series used (up to 20% difference); the facility mix is taken from the SME Review.
  5. Definition changes other than June 2019 are not quantifiable from the data.
  6. Type-level SME figures are estimates.
  7. 2013–15 SME detail comes from chart labels (one decimal place).
  8. Monthly data before December 2015 come from the compiled file and cannot be checked against an SBP archive.

Appendix CGlossary

ADR tax
Extra tax (up to 16%) on banks whose advance-to-deposit ratio is below 50% at 31 December; binding at end-2024.
CAGR
Compound annual growth rate: the constant yearly rate that takes a value from start to end.
Contribution
A segment's share of the total change in a stock between two dates.
Corporate
SBP category "other than SMEs".
EFS
Export Finance Scheme: SBP-refinanced concessional export finance; since July 2022 priced at the policy rate minus 5 points.
Facility
The SME Review's three groups: working capital, fixed investment, trade finance.
ISIC Rev. 4
UN industrial classification adopted by the SBP in June 2019.
LTFF / TERF
Long-Term Financing Facility / Temporary Economic Refinance Facility (2020–21): SBP refinance for long-term investment.
Mix
A facility's share of the SME (or corporate) loan book.
NPL ratio
Non-performing loans as a percentage of outstanding loans.
Outstanding (stock)
Balance owed on a given date.
Penetration
SME credit as a share of all business credit in a facility.
RAG
Red / amber / green flag for a cross-check gap (≤2%, ≤5%, >5%).
Series break
A jump caused by a change in measurement or classification.
SME
Small and medium enterprise as classified by banks under the SBP Prudential Regulations for SMEs.
