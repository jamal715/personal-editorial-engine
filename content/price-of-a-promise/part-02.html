Three. How finely the no-refund rule is applied matters as much as the rule itself. Applied month by month it costs 4.35 billion rupees. Applied hour by hour on the same data it costs 8.71 billion — almost exactly double. Any valuation built on monthly averages understates the liability by roughly half, and understates it in the same direction every time.

Four. Raising the fixed price is a weak defence. It reduces what the fund expects to pay in an ordinary year, but it barely touches the extreme hours that create the tail, because those hours price far above any plausible strike. Prefunding the pool more heavily does more for less.

Five. At a pool loading of 67 per cent the structure absorbs the worst twelve consecutive months anywhere in the record without calling on NCGCL. That figure is solved for, not chosen. It buys protection at an all-in price of 20,301.0 rupees per megawatt hour, against a 2025 market average of 16,350.3.

Six. In a year resembling 2025 the pool ends with 6.20 billion rupees — 45 per cent of everything collected. It is retained, not refunded. Carried into a second year it cuts the top-up required to 7.65 billion and the all-in price to 18,532.6 rupees per megawatt hour, 8.7 per cent lower. The 67 per cent is the cost of starting from nothing, not a standing surcharge.

1 · Why this exists now

For almost thirty years Pakistan bought its electricity through a single buyer. The Central Power Purchasing Agency contracted with generators on behalf of the distribution companies, and every consumer — a household in Multan, a textile mill in Faisalabad — paid a tariff that the regulator determined after the fact.1 Whatever the market cost, it was socialised. Nobody chose their supplier and nobody carried a price.

That arrangement ended in stages. The National Electric Power Regulatory Authority approved the Competitive Trading Bilateral Contracts Market in November 2020; the market operator was licensed in May 2022; a trial run followed.1 The Cabinet Committee on Energy approved the formation of an Independent System and Market Operator in October 2024, folding the market function of CPPA-G together with the National Power Control Centre into one body.2 On 20 September 2026 the federal government announced that the market was open. The following day, bidding began on the first tranche: 400 megawatts, with 800 megawatts to be released over five years, available to consumers drawing more than one megawatt.3,4

The regulator had already cleared the tolls. Use of System Charges for open-access bulk consumers were approved at between 6.23 and 19.62 rupees per unit depending on the consumer category, with a fixed grid charge of one rupee per kilowatt per month.5 Those charges sit on top of whatever the buyer pays for the energy itself.

What changed, for the buyer, is not the price level. It is who owns the variance. A mill that signs a bilateral contract indexed to the market now has an input cost that moves hour by hour with the marginal plant on the system — which in Pakistan means with the price of imported fuel, the availability of hydrology, and the weather. A finance director who could previously budget from a notified tariff now has to budget from a curve.

The competitive market did not create price risk. It moved it, from a public balance sheet onto private ones, and it did so for a class of buyer that has never had to manage it.

This is the gap the proposal addresses. NCGCL exists to use guarantees to make credit and commerce possible where the underlying risk would otherwise stop it.6 A price guarantee is not a credit guarantee, but the logic is the same one: a party better placed to absorb variance absorbs it, and charges for doing so, and the transaction that would not otherwise happen happens.

The question this paper answers is narrow and practical. If NCGCL offers such a guarantee, what does it cost, who pays it, and what is left over for NCGCL to carry?

2 · The instrument

Strip away the machinery and there are four moving parts.

A fixed price. Buyers are guaranteed a rate per unit for one year. Call it K. In the case studied here K is 16,350.30 rupees per megawatt hour. The buyer budgets on that number and only that number.

A single upfront payment. Before the year starts, buyers pay once into a common pool. Nothing is collected during the year. The pool is a bucket that is filled at the beginning and drawn down as the year goes on, earning a return on whatever is still in it.

A one-sided payout. In any hour where the market price exceeds K, the pool pays the difference on the electricity the buyer consumed in that hour. In any hour where the market price is below K, nothing happens. The buyer still pays K, and the surplus stays with the fund.

An uncapped tail. If claims in a year exhaust the pool, NCGCL pays the remainder. There is no ceiling on that obligation in the structure as currently proposed.

Four features of this deserve to be named clearly at the outset, because each of them is a decision rather than a fact.

The pool is shared. Contributions are not ring-fenced by buyer; a claim from any participant is paid from the common balance. The tenor is one year, chosen for tractability rather than for any market reason. The fund’s investment return stays in the fund rather than being distributed. And the closing balance is retained, carried into the following year rather than refunded.

Those four choices are what make the instrument a mutual rather than a policy. They also, as Section 12 shows, are what make it cheaper in year two than in year one.

What this is not

It is not a hedge in the trading sense: there is no counterparty taking the other side and no position to unwind. It is not insurance against generation failure, transmission constraint, or non-payment — only against the market price of energy. And it is not a subsidy: every rupee the pool pays out was contributed by the buyers themselves, except in the tail, where NCGCL pays.

3 · Precedent and literature

Nothing in this structure is new in isolation. Every element of it — the fixed price, the prefunding, the mutualisation, the retained surplus — exists somewhere in the world, usually with a long enough record to say what goes wrong. The combination is what is unusual, and the combination is where the risk sits.

3.1 Two-sided contracts: the British model

The closest institutional relative is the British Contract for Difference. A generator and the Low Carbon Contracts Company agree a strike price. When the market reference price falls below the strike, the LCCC pays the generator the difference. When the reference price rises above the strike, the generator pays the difference back. The contract is two-way, which means consumers who fund the shortfall in cheap years receive the surplus in dear ones.7

The scheme is large and operational: allocation round seven awarded roughly 8.4 gigawatts of offshore wind at about £91 per megawatt hour in January 2026, with a further 6.2 gigawatts in a supplementary round.7 The mechanism is well understood and the settlement is routine.

The NCGCL proposal is a one-sided version of the same idea, and Section 8 quantifies exactly what that costs. The short answer is: nearly all of it.

3.2 Prefunding: the municipal energy prepay market

American public power utilities have raised money in the tax-exempt bond market for decades to prepay for gas and, more recently, renewable energy. A bank receives bond proceeds, makes an upfront payment to a supplier, and the utility takes delivery over time at a discount.8 The savings are real and documented — the Turlock Irrigation District reported 93.3 million dollars over ten years on one transaction; CPS Energy reported eight per cent against direct power purchase agreements.8

The relevant lesson is not the tax arbitrage, which has no Pakistani equivalent. It is that a large upfront payment converts a stream of uncertain future costs into a single present one, and that the discount the payer receives is essentially the time value of the money they have handed over. The NCGCL pool works the same way in reverse: the fund earns the return on money the buyer has parted with, and that return is what allows the premium to be smaller than the claims it covers. In 2025 the pool earns 1.06 billion rupees, 12.2 per cent of the claims it pays.

The corollary, taken up in Section 14, is that the buyer’s own cost of money is the hidden term in the pricing. If they fund the premium at a rate above what the pool earns, some of the saving is illusory.

3.3 Mutualisation: the nuclear and marine pools

Mutual insurance is the oldest answer to a risk that commercial markets will not price. Nuclear Electric Insurance Limited was formed in 1980, after Three Mile Island, precisely because conventional insurers would not write the cover; it is owned by the utilities it insures and has covered essentially the whole American nuclear fleet since.9 Marine protection and indemnity clubs work on the same principle: members are simultaneously the insured and the insurer, premiums are calls rather than prices, and surpluses are retained or returned by decision of the members.10

Two features of that model carry across directly. First, retained surplus lowers future calls, which is exactly the mechanism in Section 12. Second, and less comfortably, mutuals can and do make supplementary calls when a year goes badly. The NCGCL structure as proposed has no supplementary call: the tail falls on NCGCL instead. That is a substantive difference and it is where the balance sheet risk lives.

3.4 Stabilisation funds that ran out

The cautionary literature is unambiguous and it is regional. Chile froze electricity prices in late 2019 following social unrest, reversing a 9.2 per cent increase. Distribution companies could not pay generators in full, and the unpaid balance accumulated. By February 2021 the state had to issue the region’s first tariff stabilisation bond — 489 million dollars, followed days later by a further 139 million — to buy the receivables and keep the generation sector solvent.11

Chile’s fuel price mechanism, MEPCO, has a parallel history of accumulating deferred balances that eventually had to be released, with sharp price increases when they were.12

The failure mode in both cases is the same and it is simple: a stabilisation mechanism that is not funded to the level of the stress it is meant to absorb does not eliminate the cost. It defers it, converts it into a claim on a public balance sheet, and then releases it in a lump at the worst possible moment. Pakistan has its own well-documented version of this pattern in the power sector’s circular debt, where a debt servicing surcharge of roughly 233 billion rupees was being levied on consumers as of the regulator’s 2025 State of Industry Report.13

A fund that is underpriced does not fail loudly. It works for two or three ordinary years and then arrives, all at once, as a fiscal problem.

This is the single strongest argument for a loading calibrated against the worst observed window rather than the average one, and it is the reason Section 11 treats the loading rather than the strike as the real safety lever.

3.5 What happens when nobody hedges

Winter Storm Uri in February 2021 is the standing example of unhedged retail exposure to a wholesale market. Texas prices were administratively held at the 9,000 dollar per megawatt hour cap for days. Retail customers on pass-through products received bills in the thousands; their supplier filed for bankruptcy protection, attributing it to the market’s pricing.14 Litigation over those prices continued for years.15

The instructive detail is that the extreme cost was concentrated in a very small number of hours. That concentration is precisely what makes a strike-price defence weak and a funding defence strong, and it is visible, in miniature, in the Pakistani data examined here.

3.6 The pricing theory

Two standard results from actuarial science frame everything that follows.

The first is the expected value premium principle. A premium is written as P = (1 + α) × E[X], where E[X] is the expected loss and α ≥ 0 is the loading. The loading is not profit. It compensates for the fact that claims are random and may exceed expectation, for the capital that must be held against that possibility, and for expenses.16 The 67 per cent in this paper is α, and nothing more.

The second is Jensen’s inequality, which states that for a convex function f, the expectation of f(X) is at least f of the expectation.17 The payout rule here, max(price − K, 0), is convex. Averaging prices before applying it therefore understates the payout, always, and never overstates it. Section 8 measures the size of that understatement on real data: it is a factor of two.

There is also a compact way to describe the instrument in market terms. A payout of max(price − K, 0) on a quantity of electricity in a given hour is a call option on that hour’s energy struck at K. What is being sold here is a strip of 8,760 such options, one per hour, prepaid as a bundle. This paper does not price them with an option model — there is no liquid forward curve or implied volatility surface for Pakistani hourly power to calibrate one — but the framing is worth holding, because it explains why the cost is so sensitive to how finely the hours are cut.

3.7 Where this structure is genuinely novel

Prefunded, one-sided, mutualised, with the tail on a guarantee company rather than on members, applied to hourly wholesale power in a market that opened this week. Each component has a precedent. The assembly does not, as far as the sources reviewed here show, and that is a reason for caution rather than for pride. Novel assemblies fail in the joints.

4 · The data

Everything in this paper comes from one source: the ISMO master database of hourly system demand and hourly marginal cost, covering February 2024 to April 2026.

The file is organised as month-by-hour blocks. For each calendar month there are twenty-four numbers for demand, one per hour of the day, expressed in megawatts, and twenty-four numbers for marginal cost, one per hour, expressed in rupees per kilowatt hour. Each figure is an average across all the days in that month at that hour: the entry for July at 20:00 is the mean of thirty-one readings.

Twelve months by twenty-four hours gives 288 cells of demand and 288 of price. That grid is the entire input. Section 13 returns to what is lost by holding the data at that resolution rather than at all 8,760 individual hours, because it matters more than it first appears.

Why calendar 2025

The database spans twenty-seven months, which permits sixteen overlapping twelve-month windows. Calendar 2025 is used as the base case for one reason: it is the only complete calendar year in the file, and a contract year that runs January to December is what a regulator and a finance department will both expect to see.

Rolling windows are used elsewhere, and deliberately. The loading in Section 10 is calibrated not against 2025 but against the worst twelve consecutive months anywhere in the record, which runs from May 2025 to April 2026. Presenting a structure that is calibrated on a harder window than the one displayed is the honest way round; the reverse would be circular.

The market picture

Over calendar 2025 the system averaged 14,271.91 MW, which is 125,021,932 megawatt hours of energy, or 125.02 terawatt hours. The load-weighted average marginal cost across that energy was 16,350.34 rupees per megawatt hour. The 400 MW book studied here draws 3,504,000 megawatt hours of that, and would have paid 57.29 billion rupees for it at market prices. That figure recurs throughout and is worth holding on to.
