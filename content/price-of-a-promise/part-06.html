67% · the solved level Pool loading

11 · What each lever actually does

Three inputs can be changed. They do not behave alike, and the differences are not intuitive.

Book size: exactly linear, and per-unit invariant

Everything scales with megawatts. Claims, premium, exposure, all of it. What does not change is any per-unit figure. A 250 MW book pays 250 × 34,607,821 = 8.65 billion rupees, and its all-in price is still 20,301.0 per megawatt hour. This is what makes the structure a product rather than a negotiation: the price list is per megawatt, and it is the same for everyone.

Loading: linear, and the real safety lever

Premium is expected cost times (1 + α), so the all-in price is a straight line in α. Every rupee of loading goes into the pool and stays available. Nothing about it is clever, which is precisely its merit.

Fixed price: non-linear, and much weaker than instinct suggests

Raising K reduces the premium substantially. It does much less to the tail. In the sensitivity runs behind this paper, a large increase in the strike removed only about a fifth of the worst-case exposure.

The reason is structural and is visible in the data. Most covered hours price close to K, so a shift in the strike removes them from the payout entirely — a large effect on the expected cost. The hours that generate the tail price far above any plausible strike, and moving K by a few hundred rupees per megawatt hour barely dents them. The same concentration that made Winter Storm Uri so expensive in so few hours14 makes a strike-price defence weak here.

Raising the tariff protects the average year. It does not protect the bad one. Funding the pool protects the bad one, which is the only year in which protection is needed.

This runs against the instinct of a guarantee business, where price is usually the first lever reached for. It is worth stating plainly because the analysis initially pointed the other way, and the data corrected it.

12 · The surplus, and why year two is cheaper

The 6.197 billion rupees left in the pool at the end of 2025 is not a profit and it is not a refund. It is next year’s opening balance.

If the structure is to offer the same protection in year two — the same total pool capacity — the top-up required is the difference:

target pool = PKR 13,843,128,560
carried forward = PKR 6,196,512,767
year-two top-up = PKR 7,646,615,793 = 2,182.3 PKR/MWh

implied year-two loading = 7,646,615,793 ÷ 8,289,298,538 − 1 = −7.8%
year-two all-in = 16,350.30 + 2,182.3 = 18,532.6 PKR/MWh
reduction = 1,768.4 PKR/MWh · 8.7% off the all-in price

The loading turns negative. Buyers in year two would contribute less than the expected cost of their own cover, because the fund already holds the difference.

This is the mutual argument, and it is the strongest commercial point in the structure. The 67 per cent is the price of starting from nothing. In a mutual with retained surplus, that cost is paid once by the first cohort — which raises a question of fairness that Section 14 takes up, because the first cohort capitalises the fund for everyone who follows.

Two conditions attach. Year one must actually resemble 2025; a first year like the May-2025 window leaves nothing to carry. And the arrangement only works if the surplus is genuinely retained and not distributed, which is a governance commitment rather than an arithmetic one.

13 · Limitations

The results above are only as good as five assumptions, and each of them can be wrong in a way that matters.

1. The data is 288 averages, not 8,760 hours

Every price in the model is the mean of roughly thirty same-hour readings. A single extreme day is averaged into the other thirty and largely disappears. Because the payout function is convex, this understates the liability, and it understates it always.17 Section 8 measured the same effect one level up and found a factor of two between monthly and hourly resolution; the step from 288 cells to 8,760 hours will move the number again in the same direction. The 8.71 billion should be read as a floor, not an estimate. This is the most significant limitation in the paper, and rerunning the model on the full hourly series is the single highest-value next step.

2. The buyer is assumed to consume like the country

The book is scaled from the national curve in every hour. A real industrial buyer does not consume like the national grid: a three-shift plant is far flatter, a single-shift plant is concentrated in daylight hours, and a seasonal processor may be absent for months. Aggregation at DISCO level does not reveal this — regional shapes price within about four per cent of the national curve, because aggregation washes out individual variation — but synthetic archetypes built to represent real consumption patterns span a range of roughly 2.07 times in cost. The load shape is the largest untested assumption in the model, and it cannot be resolved from published data. It requires meter data from the actual buyers, which is why the collection template is part of the accompanying materials.

3. One year of complete data, sixteen overlapping windows

The database covers twenty-seven months. Calibrating a tail against sixteen overlapping twelve-month windows drawn from twenty-seven months of history is not the same as calibrating against a distribution. The worst window observed is not the worst window possible, and nothing in the data constrains how much worse a genuine fuel shock, a currency event or a hydrological failure could be. The 67 per cent absorbs the worst thing that has happened. It does not absorb the worst thing that can.

4. Pooling does not diversify this risk

It is natural to assume that adding buyers makes the pool safer. It does not. Every participant faces the same system price on the same day, so their claims are near-perfectly correlated. The risk is systematic, not idiosyncratic, and the law of large numbers does not apply to it. What pooling provides is scale, a shared buffer and lower unit administration — real benefits, but not diversification. A larger book means a proportionally larger tail, not a smaller one.

5. The fund return is an assumption, and NCGCL bears it

The 11 per cent contributes 1.061 billion rupees to the 2025 protection. If the pool earns 8 per cent instead, it contributes 759 million, and the missing 302 million lands in the tail — which is to say, on NCGCL. Unless the product documentation says otherwise, the investment return assumption is NCGCL’s risk and not the buyer’s. Each percentage point of fund return is worth approximately 102 million rupees a year at this pool size, or about 29 rupees per megawatt hour.

Lesser caveats

  • Interest timing. Interest accrues on the opening balance for the full month before claims are deducted, which flatters the fund slightly. The effect is small — on the order of a month of carry — but it is a convention, not a fact.
  • Marginal cost is not the settlement price. The model uses ISMO’s marginal-cost series. What a bilateral contract actually settles against will be defined by the Market Commercial Code and may differ, particularly in hours where the marginal plant is constrained.
  • No credit risk. The model assumes buyers pay the premium and that the pool’s assets are sound. Neither is automatic in a sector with Pakistan’s payment history.13
  • No network charges. Use of System Charges of 6.23 to 19.62 rupees per unit5 sit on top of everything quoted here and are, for most consumer categories, larger than the guarantee itself.
  • Sequence risk within the year. The model runs one ordering of twelve months. A reordering changes the drawdown path and therefore the interest earned and the point of exhaustion.

14 · Open design questions

Five questions are not answered by the analysis and must be settled commercially before this becomes a product.

What does NCGCL earn? In the base case NCGCL carries an uncapped tail and receives nothing. There is no fee line in the model. Options include a fixed arrangement fee, a share of the pool’s investment income, or a claim on part of the retained surplus. Any of them raises the all-in price; none of them can be avoided by leaving the question open.

Who owns the retained surplus? The 6.20 billion carried into year two belongs to somebody. If a buyer leaves after year one, do they take a share? Are contributions tracked as units, or is it a common pot? Mutual insurers have well-worn answers to this,9,10 and the choice determines whether the first cohort is subsidising the second or investing in it.

Should the tail be capped? An uncapped obligation on a guarantee company’s balance sheet is a capital question before it is a pricing question. A cap, a reinsurance layer, or a supplementary call on members are the three standard answers, and mutuals typically use the third.

How is the premium financed? The buyer hands over 13.84 billion rupees on day one, and the pool earns 11 per cent on it. If the buyer’s own cost of money is materially above that, the transaction destroys value for them even while the arithmetic here looks favourable, and their true all-in price is higher than 20,301. Staged payments, or a credit facility against the premium, would address it and would change the model.

What is the regulatory treatment? The fixed price, the loading and the retained surplus are all matters a regulator may wish to approve, particularly where the instrument affects the price paid by bulk consumers in a market the regulator has just opened. Section 1’s chronology suggests the regulatory architecture is still settling.

15 · Conclusions

The proposal is workable and the arithmetic holds. Six conclusions follow from it.

The tariff carries no margin. The fixed price is the market average by construction, so the guarantee has to be paid for entirely in the premium. Any subsequent negotiation that treats K as having a cushion in it is negotiating against a number that does not exist.

The cost is the asymmetry, not the promise. A two-sided contract at this strike costs 138,233 rupees across a 57 billion rupee year. The one-sided version costs 8.71 billion. If that price is unacceptable to buyers, the lever to reach for is partial two-sidedness — a share of cheap-hour savings returning to the pool — not a higher strike.

Resolution is not a technicality. Moving from monthly to hourly settlement doubles the measured cost. Moving from 288 averaged cells to 8,760 real hours will raise it again. Any competing valuation should be checked first for what resolution it was computed at, because that single question will usually explain the difference.

Funding beats pricing. The loading is the effective safety lever; the strike is not. This is counter-intuitive in a guarantee business and it is the finding most likely to be argued with.

Nil exposure is earned, not given. The 67 per cent was solved against the worst observed window. Change the price path and the exposure line stops reading nil. The structure is calibrated, not invincible, and the limitations in Section 13 all point the same way.

It is a mutual, and mutuals get cheaper. Forty-five per cent of the premium survives an ordinary year and lowers the next one — to a negative loading and an all-in price 8.7 per cent below year one. The 67 per cent is a capitalisation charge dressed as a margin. Presenting it as a permanent surcharge would misrepresent the product in the direction least favourable to it.

What would change these conclusions, in order of how much it would move them: real hourly data; real buyer load shapes; a longer price history; and an explicit answer to what NCGCL is paid for standing behind the tail.
