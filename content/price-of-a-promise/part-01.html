Pricing a promise What it costs to fix the price of electricity for a year, and who pays when the fund runs out

On 21 September 2026 Pakistan opened bidding on its first 400 MW electricity wheeling auction. For the first time, large industrial buyers can contract directly with generators — and carry the price risk themselves. This paper prices the instrument that would take that risk back off them: a prefunded pool, one year long, backtested hour by hour on the market’s own 2025 data.

Summary of findings

Six results, each reproducible from the published data and the accompanying workbook.

One. The fixed price at the centre of the proposal, 16,350.30 rupees per megawatt hour, is not a negotiated number. It is the load-weighted average market price of calendar 2025, computed to four decimal places and then written into the contract. It contains no margin of any kind. Anything the guarantee costs must therefore be recovered somewhere else.

Two. Almost the entire cost of the product comes from one design choice: the buyer keeps the benefit of cheap hours and is protected in dear ones. Run the same contract two-sided — refunding the fund when the market prices below the fixed price — and the net cost across 2025 is 138,233 rupees on an energy bill of 57.29 billion. That is two ten-thousandths of one per cent. Run it one-sided and the cost is 8.71 billion rupees. The promise is almost free; the asymmetry is the whole price.
