The chart makes the mechanism visible. Five months — May, July, August, September and December — show a naive gap of zero and a real gap of between 342 and 2,249 rupees per megawatt hour. In every one of those months the average price sat below K, and in every one of them there were individual hours that did not. July averages 16,104 against a strike of 16,350 and still costs the fund 867 million rupees.

This is Jensen’s inequality doing its work on real data: the payout function is convex, so averaging the inputs understates the output.17 The direction of the error is not random. It is always downward, and here it is a factor of two.

The coincidence, and what it is not

That the hourly figure is almost exactly double the monthly one is arithmetic accident, not structure. The ratio depends on how much intra-month price dispersion there is, and there is no reason for it to be 2.00 in another year or another market. What is structural is the sign: the coarser method is always the lower one.

The same logic applies one level further down, and it runs against NCGCL. The 288-cell grid is itself an average of roughly thirty same-hour readings. Applying the rule to all 8,760 actual hours would raise the figure again. The 8.71 billion is a floor.

9 · The worked case, end to end

Everything above comes together in a single configuration, and it is the configuration the live model opens on. A 400 megawatt book. A fixed price of 16,350.30 rupees per megawatt hour. A pool loading of 67 per cent. A fund return of 11 per cent a year. The national load shape, and a price path in which 2025 simply repeats.

The premium follows in three lines:

expected cost = NPV of the twelve 2025 payouts at 11%/12 = PKR 8,289,298,538
premium = 8,289,298,538 × 1.67 = PKR 13,843,128,560

per unit = 13,843,128,560 ÷ 3,504,000 MWh = 3,950.7 PKR/MWh
all-in = 16,350.30 + 3,950.7 = 20,301.0 PKR/MWh · 20.3010 PKR/kWh
per MW of book = 13,843,128,560 ÷ 400 = PKR 34,607,821 per MW

Four things in that table are worth reading slowly.

February and November pay nothing at all. Not a reduced amount — nothing. In both months every hour priced below the fixed price. The buyer paid 16,350.30 anyway, and the pool grew by its interest alone. This is what the guarantee looks like from the buyer’s side in a cheap month, and it is the honest answer to anyone who asks what they are getting: in those months, nothing. They bought certainty, and certainty in a cheap month looks like a loss.

The two largest claims arrive first. January at 1.479 billion and April at 1.803 billion, together 37.7 per cent of the year’s claims, inside the first four months. Because the pool is drawn in sequence, this is the harder ordering. The same twelve claims with the heavy months at the end would leave a larger average balance and earn more interest.

The pool earns 1.061 billion rupees. That is 12.2 per cent of what it pays out. The return is not decoration; it is a material component of the protection, and Section 14 notes that the assumption behind it is NCGCL’s risk and not the buyer’s.

The closing balance is 6.197 billion. Forty-five per cent of everything collected. It does not refund.

10 · Reverse-engineering the pool: where 67 per cent comes from

The loading is the only number in the structure that looks arbitrary, and it is the one number that is not.

Start from the wrong answer. Charge the expected cost exactly — a loading of zero — and the buyer pays 8.289 billion rupees, or 2,365.7 per megawatt hour, for an all-in price of 18,716.0. In a repeat of 2025 that works: the pool pays every claim and finishes at almost precisely zero.

But 2025 is not the question. The question is what happens in a bad year, and the record contains one. Scanning all sixteen overlapping twelve-month windows in the database, the worst runs from May 2025 to April 2026, where claims at this book size reach approximately 14.98 billion rupees — about 1.7 times the calendar-2025 figure. At zero loading, that window costs NCGCL roughly 6.2 billion rupees, or about 15.5 million per megawatt of book.

The loading is then defined by a question rather than chosen by judgement: at what multiple of expected cost does the premium, plus the interest it earns while being drawn down, exactly absorb the worst window in the record?

solve α  such that  expected cost × (1 + α)  +  interest earned  =  worst-window claims

The answer is α = 0.67. Not a round number, because it was not picked; it is where NCGCL’s cheque goes to zero in the hardest twelve months the market has produced.

The relationship is close to linear on both sides, which gives two rules of thumb that hold well enough for a meeting.

every 10 points of loading → +236.6 PKR/MWh on the all-in price
every 10 points of loading → ≈ −0.92bn of worst-case exposure
— so one percentage point of loading buys roughly 92 million rupees of protection for 23.7 PKR/MWh. The first line is exact; the second is a straight line drawn between two computed points

That is the trade in its simplest form, and it is the trade the board is actually being asked to make.
